There are three words you need to learn to understand your DNA - Genes, SNPs, and alleles. Let's explain them images, videos and a few words.

What is a Gene?

Your DNA is a molecule shaped like a twisted ladder — the famous double helix — and it runs about three billion lines long. Stretched out, the DNA in a single human cell would be roughly two metres. It's packed into 23 pairs of chromosomes, tucked into the nucleus of almost every cell in your body.

A gene is a specific stretch of that DNA with a particular sequence that carries the instructions for making something useful — usually a protein. Proteins do almost everything in the body: they form structures, carry molecules, speed up chemical reactions, and regulate other genes. You have roughly 20,000–25,000 genes, though they only account for about 2% of your total DNA. The rest regulates them, or hasfunctions we're still figuring out.

Think of it like in a sentence, most of the sentence is just grammar, "a", "the", "very", but sometimes there are context words, "parks", "bridge", "walk". If I say, "I am going to the beach", or I say, "going beach" they both carry the same meaning but "I" "am' "to" and "the" allow the actual movement of the sentence and keep everything in place. In a sentence "going" and 'beach" are like the 2% of your DNA we know are a part of the human blueprint creating differences for how your body functions.

What is an SNP?

DNA is written in a similar way to your computer. Computers work in binary, which is 0 and 1s.

So when you type in "a", the computer stores that in computer's language as "01100001", for the number 23 it stores it as "00010111", the computer only has 0 and 1 to create things with.

Thats why you can change your language in your computer to Chinese and it will still be able to understand your inputs - because it has its own non-spoken language code.

DNA uses a four-letter alphabet: "A", "T", "C", and "G" which are acronyms for nucleotides - Adenine, Cytosine, Guanine, and Thymine.

So ACGT in a row or AACCTTGGA in a row means something different, or AGGCCCAAAAAA, a neverending sequence writing things that is a blueprint for actions to take.

If this blueprint changes sequence for some reason, we call this a SNP, a new command inside the blueprints.

Single Nucleotide Polymorphism — pronounced "snip", abbreviated SNP — is a single-letter swap at one position in the sequence. For example, where most people have a C in the sequence of AGTTCGGA, you might have a T. That one-letter difference is a/an SNP.

The human genome has roughly three billion base pair positions. The vast majority of those positions are fixed — every human alive has exactly the same letter there. No variation, nothing to note. A position only becomes an SNP when a second nucleotides (allele) has started to bind to that same section at some point in human history and spread widely enough through the population to be considered meaningful. Meaning at least 1 in 100 people (>1% of the human population) carry the alternate version:

Fixed position 100% G in every human not an SNP — nothing varies
Rare variant G 99.8% · A 0.2% catalogued, but not a common SNP
Common SNP G 57% · A 43% SNP change noted and given a number like rs2236225

SNPs are incredibly common — but to appreciate the scale:

Your body contains roughly 37 trillion cells.

Almost every one of them holds a complete copy of your genome: 6.4 billion base pairs of DNA (3.2 billion from each parent), packed into 46 chromosomes.

Across that 6.4 billion base pair sequence, humans differ from each other at roughly 4–5 million positions — that's where your personal SNPs sit, you will not have all 4-5 million possible human SNPs, but you will have many of them.

Of all the SNPs catalogued across the global population in NCBI's dbSNP database, about 10 million are considered common (minor allele frequency >1% of humans).

Any given person carries around 3–5 million variants each relative to the reference genome, but only 50,000–100,000 fall inside actual coding gene regions. Meaning most of those differences are completely silent — they sit in regions that don't code for anything critical, or the swap doesn't change how it's area fully operates.

But some SNPs matter a great deal. For example, the "MTHFR C677T" gene variant, SNP rs1801133, is a single C→T swap that reduces it's processing of folate efficiency by 30–70%.

Each SNP has a unique reference ID called an rsID (reference SNP ID), assigned by the NCBI dbSNP database. These look like rs1801133, rs1805087, rs4846048. They're how researchers and databases refer to specific positions in the genome consistently across studies.

What is an Allele?

An allele is the specific version of a gene — or a specific letter at a position — that you actually carry. Because you have two copies of every chromosome, you have two alleles at every position: one inherited from your mother, one from your father.

If both copies are the same (e.g. CC at a given SNP position), you're homozygous at that position. If they differ (e.g. CT), you're heterozygous. This matters because many variants only show significant effects when homozygous — carrying two copies of the less common allele. Heterozygous carriers often land somewhere in between.

A dominant allele expresses itself even when only one copy is present. A recessive allele only shows its effect when both copies carry it — one from each parent. The classic way to visualise this is through trait inheritance in a real organism.

What makes genetics genuinely surprising is epistasis — where one gene masks or modifies the effect of another entirely. The classic example is coat colour in mice. Two separate genes are at play: one controls whether the coat will be black (B, dominant) or brown (b, recessive), and a second controls whether any pigment is produced at all (C = colour present, c = no colour). A mouse that is cc (homozygous for no-colour) cannot make pigment regardless of what its B/b alleles say — so it appears white, even though it's carrying functional black or brown coat genes that simply can't be expressed.

Most SNPs studied in modern genetics don't follow simple dominant/recessive rules — they're additive (each copy of the variant adds a small, measurable effect) or codominant (both alleles contribute independently). Epistasis — where one gene's effect depends on what's happening at another — is why studying SNPs in isolation only tells part of the story.

What Binds to Alleles?

DNA isn't just passively sitting there. It's constantly being read, regulated, switched on and off. Several types of molecules interact with it directly.

Transcription factors are proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences near a gene and either activate or suppress its expression. Think of them as switches. A small change in the binding site sequence — from an SNP, for example — can mean a transcription factor no longer recognises it, changing how much of a protein gets made.

Methylation is a chemical modification where a methyl group (CH₃) is attached directly to a cytosine base in the DNA. Methylated regions are generally silenced — the gene doesn't get read. This is part of epigenetics: changes in gene expression that don't alter the underlying DNA sequence. Crucially, methylation depends heavily on folate metabolism. Folate (vitamin B9) feeds into a pathway that produces SAM (S-adenosylmethionine), the universal methyl donor. If your MTHFR or MTR enzymes are impaired, SAM production drops and methylation across the whole genome is affected.

This is why the MTHFR gene matters so much in folate research. Poor methylation from MTHFR variants doesn't just affect one gene — it affects gene expression across the whole genome, and influences everything from neurotransmitter synthesis to DNA repair.

Hormones such as oestrogen, testosterone, and cortisol bind to receptor proteins that then act as transcription factors. This is why hormone levels can have broad effects on gene expression, and why SNPs in receptor genes (like oestrogen receptor alpha, ESR1) can alter sensitivity to hormonal signals.

Drugs and antifolates work by mimicking or blocking the natural molecules that bind to gene products. Methotrexate, for instance, blocks DHFR (dihydrofolate reductase) by fitting into the same binding site as dihydrofolate — jamming the folate cycle and stopping rapidly dividing cells. Your SNPs in DHFR, TYMS, and related genes affect how well (or poorly) antifolate drugs work in your body.

What is a Haplogroup?

As humans migrated out of Africa over tens of thousands of years, populations became geographically separated. Over time, each group accumulated its own set of SNPs — small mutations that arose and spread within that group but not others. A haplogroup is a cluster of such SNPs that are reliably inherited together (because they sit close together on the chromosome and rarely get separated by recombination). It acts as a genetic fingerprint for an ancestral lineage.

There are two main types. Mitochondrial haplogroups trace your maternal line: mitochondrial DNA is passed only from mother to child, so it accumulates mutations linearly through the maternal lineage without being shuffled by recombination. Mitochondrial haplogroups have letter-based names like H, J, T, U — H is the most common in Europeans. Y-chromosome haplogroups trace the paternal line in men, in the same way.

Haplogroups don't tell you what your genes do — they tell you where your ancestors came from. They're population genetics, not functional genetics. Knowing you're haplogroup H1a tells you about migration patterns 10,000 years ago, not about your health.

Where haplogroups become relevant to functional genetics is in population frequency data. An SNP that's common in one haplogroup may be rare in another, which affects how we interpret its significance. This is why this site uses Caucasian population frequencies from NCBI as the reference — it's the population context that matches the data.

Where the Data Lives

Several public databases underpin modern genetics research. Each serves a slightly different purpose — from canonical variant registration to clinical interpretation to population frequency analysis. This site draws on all of them.

NCBI dbSNP ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/snp

The authoritative registry for every known SNP. Run by the National Center for Biotechnology Information, it assigns each variant its rsID — the universal naming system used across all genetics research globally. The source of allele frequencies, consequence annotations (synonymous, missense, stop-gained), and chromosomal coordinates.

rs2236225 — example SNP ↗
SNPedia snpedia.com

A community-maintained wiki of clinical SNP interpretations. Each rsID page carries a magnitude score (0–10) summarising the strength of associated evidence, links to supporting studies, and plain-language summaries of what the variant is thought to do. Less formal than ClinVar, but often more readable — and frequently updated faster.

rs2236225 — example SNP ↗
GeneCards genecards.org

A gene-centric portal from the Weizmann Institute of Science. Each card aggregates data from over 150 sources into one page — known variants, protein structure, expression patterns by tissue, disease associations, drug interactions, and pathway memberships. A good first stop when you want the full picture around a specific gene rather than a specific variant.

MTHFD1 · rs2236225 — example ↗
gnomAD gnomad.broadinstitute.org

The Genome Aggregation Database, produced by the Broad Institute. Contains whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing data from over 125,000 individuals across diverse ancestry groups — making it the gold standard for population-level allele frequency data. Particularly valuable for understanding how rare or common a variant is in specific ancestral populations, and whether it's ever been observed in a healthy cohort.

gnomad.broadinstitute.org ↗
OMIM omim.org

Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man — the authoritative catalogue of human genes and genetic disorders, continuously updated since 1966. Each entry links a gene or locus to its known phenotypic effects, with evidence classifications and references to the original discovery literature. The bedrock resource for understanding gene–disease relationships.

omim.org ↗
ClinVar ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar

NCBI's database of clinically interpreted variants, aggregating submissions from diagnostic laboratories, research groups, and clinicians worldwide. Each variant receives a clinical significance rating — pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, or uncertain — based on the cumulative weight of evidence submitted. Essential for understanding whether a specific variant has a formally recognised disease connection.
